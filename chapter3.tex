\section{Experimental Setup and Datasets}
\label{sec:experimental_setup}

To rigorously evaluate the proposed multi-year architectures against the established single-year baselines,
a standardized experimental framework was designed. All models were trained, validated,
and tested using identical data splits to ensure a strictly fair comparative analysis.

\subsection{The INVEKOS Dataset and Sentinel-2}
\label{subsec:invekos_sentinel2}

To evaluate the proposed sequence-modeling architectures, experiments were conducted using the
Austrian \textbf{INVEKOS} (Integrated Administration and Control System) dataset \cite{invekos_austria}.
INVEKOS serves as the European Union's official spatial registry for agricultural parcel identification, providing rich,
multi-year ground-truth vector data based on farmers' declarations.
To facilitate multi-year sequence modeling, the dataset was preprocessed to isolate a strict temporal sequence,
tracking crop rotations across six consecutive harvest years (2017 to 2022).
Fields that did not maintain a consistent presence across all six years were discarded, ensuring a continuous temporal footprint.
After filtering, the dataset encompasses \textbf{47 distinct crop classes}, ranging from dominant cereals (e.g., winter wheat, maize) to specialized regional vegetation.

The input features are constructed from Earth Observation data provided by the European Space Agency's \textbf{Sentinel-2}mission \cite{drusch_sentinel2_2012}.
Sentinel-2 provides 13 multi-spectral bands with a spatial resolution of up to 10 meters and a frequent 5-day revisit time.
However, operationalizing this data presents severe challenges due to persistent cloud cover and atmospheric interference over the Austrian Alps,
resulting in highly irregular temporal sampling.

It is important to explicitly state that the data extraction and pre-processing pipeline utilized in this thesis was originally designed and
implemented by Perantoni et al. \cite{perantoni_bayesian_2024}. By adopting their highly optimized dataset structure,
this research was able to bypass standard preprocessing bottlenecks and focus entirely on the architectural development of neural network sequence models. 

\subsection{HPC Environment and 1D Pixel-Based Extraction}
\label{subsec:hpc_environment}

Training deep sequence models on massive, high-dimensional satellite time series is computationally demanding.
To facilitate this, all experiments were executed on a dedicated High-Performance Computing (HPC) node equipped with an AMD Ryzen 7 5800X processor,
67GB of system RAM, and an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3060 GPU, running on an Ubuntu 22.04 LTS environment.

A primary challenge in processing the INVEKOS dataset is the severe input/output (I/O) bottleneck caused by loading thousands of multi-year temporal sequences.
Initial exploratory experiments attempted to utilize the full 2D spatial patches (e.g., $32 \times 32$ pixels across time) of the agricultural parcels.
Because the dataset was exceptionally large (approximately 1.3 TB), it could not be stored on the
HPC node's local solid-state drive (SSD) and instead had to be hosted on a shared network folder.
The immense volume of network-based disk reads required to process these 2D geometries caused severe GPU starvation,
rendering the training process computationally infeasible in a reasonable amount of time.

To resolve this, the experimental framework was adapted to utilize an optimized \textbf{1D pixel-based data pipeline} provided by Perantoni et al. \cite{perantoni_bayesian_2024}.
Rather than processing broad spatial context, the dataset extraction focuses entirely on the deep temporal evolution of individual pixels.
Each sample is shaped as a 1D sequence containing the 13 spectral bands measured across the specific agricultural days of the year.
This aggressive dimensionality reduction completely bypasses hardware memory limits,
sustaining high GPU utilization without the latency overhead of 2D spatial patches.

\subsection{Data Loading Strategies}
\label{subsec:data_loading}

To properly evaluate the impact of multi-year memory, the experimental framework utilized two distinct data loading
strategies tailored to the architectural needs of the models. 

For the single-year baseline models (1D-CNN and standalone Transformers), the dataset was structured using an \textbf{intra-year loader}.
This loader decoupled the temporal sequence of the dataset, extracting and serving only a single growing season at a time.
This constrained the baselines to rely entirely on localized, intra-year phenological signatures for classification.

In contrast, the hybrid architectures required a full sequence view of the crop rotation cycle.
To facilitate this, an \textbf{inter-year data loader} was engineered to serve the entire continuous six-year sequence simultaneously.
This explicit separation in data handling guarantees that any performance delta observed between the architectures is strictly a product
of the hybrid models' multi-year memory capabilities.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}
\label{subsec:evaluation_metrics}

Agricultural land cover datasets are inherently unbalanced; a few dominant crop types often account for the vast majority of cultivated land,
while specialized crops represent a tiny fraction of the dataset.
Consequently, relying solely on overall accuracy can yield highly misleading evaluations.
To provide a comprehensive assessment, the models were evaluated using two primary metrics: \textbf{Overall Accuracy} and the \textbf{Macro F1-Score}.

\textbf{Overall Accuracy (OA)} measures the total proportion of correctly classified agricultural parcels across all available classes. It is defined as:
$$ \text{OA} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{C} TP_i}{N} $$
where $C$ is the total number of target classes (47 in this study), $TP_i$ represents the True Positives for class $i$, and $N$ is the total number of samples.

\textbf{Macro F1-Score} is the primary metric used to evaluate true model performance. The F1-score for a single class is the harmonic mean of Precision and Recall.
To compute the Macro F1-score, the F1-score is calculated independently for each of the 47 classes and then unweightedly averaged:
$$ \text{Macro F1} = \frac{1}{C} \sum_{i=1}^{C} \left( 2 \times \frac{\text{Precision}_i \times \text{Recall}_i}{\text{Precision}_i + \text{Recall}_i} \right) $$
Unlike a weighted average, the Macro F1-score treats every class equally, regardless of its support size.
A model can only achieve a high Macro F1-score if it successfully identifies both the dominant and the minority crop classes,
making it a rigorous benchmark for this highly imbalanced dataset.


\section{Infrastructure and MLOps}
\label{sec:infrastructure_mlops}

The complexity of simultaneously training intra-year feature extractors and inter-year sequence heads required a robust, highly parallelized infrastructure.

\subsection{Training Framework and Optimization}
The entire experimental codebase was developed in Python utilizing the \textbf{PyTorch} deep learning framework,
wrapped within \textbf{PyTorch Lightning} \cite{falcon2019pytorch}.
PyTorch Lightning was selected to streamline the development process;
it automatically manages the underlying complexities of distributed training,
safely handling hardware scaling and the synchronization of multi-GPU workloads.

The networks were trained using the \textbf{AdamW optimizer} \cite{loshchilov2017decoupled},
which explicitly decouples weight decay from the gradient updates to improve generalization.
A crucial optimization strategy for the Transformer-HMM cascade was the implementation of decoupled learning rates.
By assigning independent learning rates to the neural backbone and the sequence head, the automated tuner could independently scale the weight updates,
ensuring the neural network learned effectively without destabilizing the HMM's probabilistic transition matrices.
All models were optimized against a Categorical Cross-Entropy (CCE) loss function.

\subsection{Automated Hyperparameter Tuning}
To ensure all baseline and hybrid models were compared fairly at their absolute peak capacity, manual tuning was abandoned in favor
of an automated Machine Learning Operations (MLOps) pipeline.
\textbf{Ray Tune} \cite{liaw2018tune}, integrated with the \textbf{Optuna} search algorithm \cite{akiba2019optuna},
was deployed to conduct distributed hyperparameter sweeps.
This combination utilized Bayesian optimization to intelligently search the hyperparameter space (learning rates, dropout ratios,
hidden dimensions, and attention heads), aggressively pruning unpromising configurations.
Furthermore, all model executions, configurations, and resulting performance metrics were systematically tracked and versioned
using a centralized \textbf{MLflow} server \cite{zaharia2018accelerating}.

\subsection{Temporal 1D-CNN vs. Transformer Encoders}
The \textbf{1D-CNN} established a strong baseline by effectively mapping local spectral variations.
However, as shown in Table \ref{tab:baseline_comparison}, it achieved a moderate overall accuracy of 55.30\% but
struggled significantly with class imbalance, recording a mean Macro F1-score of only 30.17\%.
The model's sliding receptive field likely struggled to bridge the massive temporal gaps caused by prolonged winter cloud cover.

The standalone \textbf{Transformer Encoders} successfully overcame these localized limitations.
By leveraging the AgroDays Positional Encoding and full self-attention, the Transformers mapped global dependencies across the entire growing season.
Furthermore, the explicit comparison between the two Transformer formulations yielded critical insights.
The \textbf{Discriminative Variant} achieved the highest standalone classification performance (33.18\% F1). 
In contrast, the standalone \textbf{Generative Variant} yielded a lower predictive performance (TBD\% F1). 
This drop is an expected consequence of its mathematical formulation.
By removing the bias terms and enforcing a strict, normalized probability space,
the Generative Transformer cannot default to predicting majority classes to inflate its accuracy.
While this makes it a less effective standalone classifier, this strict normalization prevents the model from generating overconfident predictions,
theoretically making it the optimal, unbiased emission model for downstream probabilistic networks.

\subsection{The Necessity of Non-Linearity: Linear Controls}
To validate the architectural necessity of the deep neural backbones, the multi-year sequence heads were deliberately paired with a \textbf{simplistic linear backbone}.
In these control configurations, the raw 1D SITS data was processed through a single linear projection before being passed to either the HMM or the LSTM. 

As hypothesized, replacing the non-linear feature extractors caused a massive degradation in performance,
but it also revealed a disparity between how the two sequence heads handle low-quality inputs.
The \textbf{Linear-HMM} configuration managed to maintain a baseline level of predictive power (5.23\% F1-score and 41.52\% Accuracy).
Because the HMM relies on an explicit, mathematically rigid transition matrix, this agronomic "safety net" allowed it to partially smooth over the highly overlapping,
low-confidence emission probabilities generated by the linear layer. 

In contrast, the \textbf{Linear-LSTM} configuration failed catastrophically, collapsing to an F1-score of barely TBD\%.
Lacking the predefined agronomic matrices of the HMM, the LSTM attempts to learn the complex, non-linear rules of crop rotation entirely end-to-end.
However, because the linear projection was mathematically incapable of untangling the spectral signatures of 47 overlapping crop classes,
the LSTM was fed near-random temporal features, completely preventing its recurrent gates from optimizing.
These control tests explicitly prove that deep, non-linear feature extractors (such as the Transformers) are an absolute prerequisite
for processing raw SITS data before any complex multi-year sequence modeling can be applied.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The experimental results validate the central premise of this research: relying solely on intra-year phenology severely
limits crop classification performance, particularly on highly imbalanced datasets.

\subsection{Single-Year vs. Multi-Year Capabilities}
\label{subsec:single_vs_multi_year}

The massive improvement from the single-year Transformers to the multi-year hybrids highlights a fundamental limitation
of traditional models: they evaluate each growing season in a vacuum.
In agricultural reality, planting decisions are heavily governed by strict multi-year crop rotation practices designed to prevent soil depletion.
By feeding a sequence of yearly embeddings into the inter-year sequence heads (LSTM or HMM), the network learns these transitional probabilities.
If the network is uncertain about a crop's spectral signature in year $t$, it can mathematically infer the likely crop by analyzing the confident
predictions from years $t-1$ and $t+1$.

\subsection{The Impact of Class Imbalance}
\label{subsec:class_imbalance}

The INVEKOS dataset suffers from severe long-tail class imbalance, dominated by staple crops. This imbalance is fully exposed by the baseline models.
The 1D-CNN achieved an overall accuracy of 55.30\%, but an F1-score of only 30.17\%.
This discrepancy indicates a "lazy" network: the model minimizes its training loss by over-predicting the majority classes
and effectively ignoring the minority crops \cite{johnson2019survey}.

The hybrid multi-year models significantly mitigate this failure mode.
While the LSTM's overall accuracy rose to TBD\%, its F1-score jumped disproportionately higher to TBD\%. This proves that
multi-year memory explicitly helps rescue minority classes. Rare crops are often planted as transitional phases within a longer crop rotation cycle.
By recognizing the complete multi-year cycle, the LSTM is forced to predict the correct minority class to satisfy the learned rotational sequence,
preventing the model from defaulting to the statistically safe, majority-class prediction.

\subsection{Computational Efficiency}
\label{subsec:comp_efficiency}

Despite its architectural complexity, the Hierarchical Transformer-LSTM proved to be highly computationally efficient during training.
This efficiency is attributed to the model's hierarchical design.
Self-attention mechanisms allow for significantly more parallelization than processing deep temporal time steps sequentially through a recurrent network.
By leveraging the Intra-Year Transformer to evaluate the dense intra-year sequences concurrently,
the sequential computation burden on the inter-year LSTM is reduced to a sequence length of merely six steps (representing the six years of data).
This guarantees that fusing intra-year attention with inter-year recurrence is not only highly accurate but also highly scalable for operational agricultural monitoring.
